PART A — FIRM AND ROLE PROFILE
======================================================================
Firm: HV Capital, European multistage venture capital investor, investing from seed through IPO with venture and growth strategies.
Per its website: about 2.8 billion euros AUM; more than 290 investments across industries; about 30 investment professionals across venture and growth.
Tickets: venture fund about 0.5 to 10 million euros (seed, Series A); growth fund up to about 60 million (Series B and later), follow-ons up to about 100 million.
Portfolio examples named on its website: Zalando, Flix, Depop, Neura Robotics, Upvest, Quantum Systems, Isar Aerospace, SumUp.
Approach as described by HV Capital: "Too new is our baseline", "Too bold is our reason", "Too complex is our edge": backing unusually novel, ambitious founders and complex technologies, and supporting them from early stage to public markets.

Interview format: VC interviews typically combine fit, thesis and deal discussion, a case study, and conversations with partners; the exact process varies. The candidate should confirm with the recruiter.

What a strong "why HV Capital" answer references (debrief only):
- Multistage model: seed to growth, so the team learns both early-stage judgment and growth metrics.
- Portfolio breadth across consumer, fintech, deep tech (robotics, aerospace, defense tech).
- Comfort with complex technology and a personal edge to source or judge it.
Weak: "you're a top fund", "I love startups".

PART B — HOW TO PROBE A CANDIDATE'S BACKGROUND
======================================================================
Pick the one or two CV items most relevant to this role. Never recite the CV. For each item choose two or three probes.

ANY ROLE
- What did you personally own or decide, as opposed to the team?
- What was the result, in a number?
- What would you do differently?
- How does this make you better at venture investing?

PRIOR PE, VC, GROWTH, OR ANGEL EXPERIENCE (internship, scout, student fund)
- Walk me through one deal you worked on or screened. Would you have invested? At what price?
- Tell me about one company you'd have backed and one you'd have passed on, and why.
- What did you learn about how a fund actually makes decisions?
- What was the best memo or market map you produced, and did it change anyone's mind?

INVESTMENT BANKING, M&A, TRANSACTION SERVICES
- Walk me through a deal: what was the investment case for the buyer, and would you have bought it?
- What did the model miss?

CONSULTING, ADVISORY, STRATEGY, DUE DILIGENCE
- Which project taught you the most about what makes a business valuable?
- If you'd had to invest your own money in that client, would you?

STARTUP OR OPERATOR EXPERIENCE (employee or founder)
- What was the company's biggest risk, and what did you do about it?
- Founders: pitch it in 60 seconds. Would you invest in it as an investor? Why or why not?

STUDENT INVESTMENT CLUBS, COMMUNITY BUILDING
- What did you build or change, not just join? How did you select people or deals?

RESEARCH, TECHNICAL, OR ENGINEERING BACKGROUND
- Which companies could be built on this technology, and which would you back?
- How do you judge a technical team outside your own field?

SIDE PROJECTS, MARKET MODELS, OWN INVESTMENTS
- Walk me through the key assumption in your model. What happens if it's off by half?
- Best and worst investment you've made yourself, and what you learned.

NO DIRECTLY RELEVANT EXPERIENCE OR CAREER SWITCHER
- What made you decide on this path, and what have you done to test that decision?
- What do you bring that a typical candidate doesn't?

PART C — QUESTION BANK
(The "strong answer" notes are for the debrief only.)
======================================================================
MOTIVATION AND FIT
1. Walk me through your background in two minutes.
   Strong: chronological, crisp, each step explains the next, ends with why VC now. Under two minutes.
2. Why VC and not founding or joining a startup?
   Strong: honest, specific; shows they understand the job (sourcing, judgment, saying no a lot, long feedback loops).
3. Why a multistage fund, and why HV Capital?
4. What's your edge as an investor, given you don't have a long track record?
   Strong: a concrete edge from their background plus honest self-awareness about gaps.
5. Which investor do you admire and why?

MARKET AND THESIS
6. Pick a startup you'd invest in today. Pitch it to me.
   Strong: problem, solution, why now, market size with logic, team, traction, competition, risks, what you'd need to believe, a clear decision.
7. Which sector are you most excited about for the next five years, and what's your thesis?
8. What's overhyped right now?
9. Humanoid robots: venture-scale opportunity or capital trap?
   Strong: separates hardware vs software layers, capex and margins, deployment timeline, who captures value, early proof points.
10. Where in the AI stack would you invest as a European fund, and where would you avoid?
   Strong: capital intensity of foundation models, vertical applications, data moats, workflow ownership, distribution.

SOURCING AND VALUE-ADD
11. How would you find the best founders before other funds do?
   Strong: specific channels (university spin-offs and incubators, research labs, technical communities, conference talks, operator networks), a repeatable system, how they'd win the deal.
12. You're the intern. How do you add value in your first month?
13. A founder you like gets a term sheet from a bigger fund. What do you do?

CURVEBALLS (use one)
14. Tell me about an investment decision you'd have gotten wrong.
15. What's something you believe about technology that most people disagree with?
16. If I gave you 1 million euros to invest today, what would you do?
17. What would make you a bad VC?
18. Sell our fund in 30 seconds as if I were a founder.

PART D — QUANT BANK WITH ANSWER KEYS
======================================================================
Q1 Seed math: a startup raises 2 million at 8 million pre-money. Post-money and investor ownership?
Answer: post-money 10 million; investor 20 percent.

Q2 Dilution: then a Series A raises 6 million at 24 million pre-money. What does the seed investor own after?
Answer: post 30 million; Series A owns 20 percent; everyone else diluted by 0.8; seed goes from 20 to 16 percent; founders from 80 to 64 percent.

Q3 Option pool shuffle: seed of 2 million at 8 million pre, with a 10 percent post-money option pool created in the pre-money. Founder ownership and effective pre-money?
Answer: investor 20 percent, pool 10 percent, founders 70 percent; effective pre-money for founders 7 million.

Q4 Fund returner: a 100 million fund owns 8 percent at exit. What exit value returns the fund?
Answer: 1.25 billion.

Q5 Liquidation preference: 5 million for 25 percent, 1x non-participating.
Exit 12 million: takes 5 million. Exit 40 million: converts, gets 10 million. Indifference point 20 million. If 1x participating at 40 million: 5 + 25 percent of 35 = 13.75 million.

Q6 Unit economics: ARPA 625 euros a month, 80 percent gross margin, 3 percent monthly churn, CAC 4,000 euros.
Answer: LTV about 16,700; LTV/CAC about 4.2; payback 8 months; about 31 percent annual logo churn.

Q7 Fund economics: a 150 million fund wants 3x net to LPs with 20 percent carry, ignoring fees. Gross proceeds needed?
Answer: 150 + 0.8 x (G - 150) = 450, so G = 525 million. With about 30 million of fees, only about 120 million is invested: about 4.4x gross on invested capital. The power law.

Q8 Burn multiple: net burn 4 million, net new ARR 2 million.
Answer: 2.0. Around 1 or below is efficient.

Q9 Valuation sanity: Series A at 30 million post with 2 million ARR.
Answer: 15x ARR; needs fast growth, strong retention, large market.

Q10 Ownership: why do early-stage funds target meaningful ownership (often 10 to 20 percent)?
Answer: so one winner can return the fund after dilution.

PART E — LIVE CASES (fictional companies)
Pitch the headline in about four sentences. Reveal facts only when asked. If asked something not listed, say the founders didn't share it.

CASE 1 — FUGENWERK ROBOTICS (industrial robotics, seed)
Headline: Munich startup selling robotic welding cells with no-code programming to Mittelstand metalworking shops. Raising 3 million euros at 12 million pre-money. 600 thousand euros annual recurring revenue.
Facts on request:
- CTO: ex-robotics engineer at a large robot maker (10 years); CEO: ex-strategy consultant.
- Hardware cell about 120 thousand euros plus software at 18 thousand euros per cell per year.
- 9 customers; the largest is 40 percent of revenue.
- Blended gross margin 35 percent (software about 80 percent).
- Sales cycle about 9 months. Burn 180 thousand euros a month; 5 months of runway without the round.
- Competition: robot makers' own software, cobot welding startups, systems integrators.
Debrief notes: green flags: real pain (welder shortage), domain-expert founder, paying customers, software attach. Red flags: concentration, low blended margin, long sales cycle, short runway, hardware capital intensity.

CASE 2 — LEDGERLOOP (applied AI, pre-seed)
Headline: Berlin startup automating accounts payable for small and mid-sized businesses with AI. Raising 1.5 million at 6 million pre-money. 25 thousand euros monthly recurring revenue, growing 15 percent month over month.
Facts on request:
- 40 paying customers; 625 euros per customer per month; 3 percent monthly churn; CAC about 4,000 euros; gross margin 80 percent.
- Two founders, ex-fintech product managers; no ML specialist yet.
- Distribution mostly via tax-advisor referrals.
- Competition: accounting software adding AI, funded AP-automation startups.
Debrief notes: strong growth and unit economics (LTV/CAC about 4.2, payback 8 months) but about 31 percent annual logo churn, thin moat, channel dependence.

CASE 3 — KATHODE KREISLAUF (battery recycling, Series A)
Headline: University spin-off recovering lithium, nickel, and cobalt from used EV batteries. Raising 15 million at 45 million pre-money. Pilot plant running, pre-revenue.
Facts on request:
- Pilot recovery rates above 90 percent; not yet proven at commercial scale.
- Non-binding letters of intent with two cathode producers.
- Next step: a commercial plant needing about 80 million, financed with project debt, grants, and strategic capital.
- EU rules require minimum recycled content in new batteries over the next decade.
- Risks: metal prices, feedstock supply only later, established recyclers.
Debrief notes: tests capital-intensity awareness, financing stack, dilution, and the gap between letters of intent and offtake contracts.

CASE 4 — SWARMLINE (robot orchestration software, seed)
Headline: Software that coordinates mixed fleets of mobile robots from different vendors in warehouses. Raising 4 million at 16 million pre-money. Three pilots, one converted to a 150 thousand euro per year contract.
Facts on request:
- Integrates via the VDA 5050 interface standard plus custom adapters.
- Pricing per robot per month. Founders: ex-logistics automation engineers.
- Competition: robot makers' fleet managers, warehouse management software vendors, integrators.
- Integration takes 3 to 4 months per site.
Debrief notes: tests lock-in by robot makers, services vs software margin, whether interoperability is a product or a feature, pilot-to-contract conversion. If the candidate has a similar idea of their own, ask whether that makes them more or less likely to invest.

PART F — SCORING RUBRIC (1 to 5)
======================================================================
Motivation and fit: 1 generic ("I love startups"); 3 reasonable but not fund-specific; 5 specific, credible edge tied to the fund's model.
Structured thinking: 1 rambling; 3 some structure; 5 structure upfront, top-down, concise.
Investment judgment: 1 no decision or only positives; 3 decision with some risks; 5 clear decision, key risk, "what I'd need to believe", what would change their mind.
Quant accuracy: 1 wrong and lost; 3 right with help; 5 fast, correct, explains implications.
Communication: 1 long, filler-heavy; 3 clear but long; 5 crisp, headline first, under 90 seconds per answer.
Conviction under pushback: 1 folds or gets defensive; 3 holds but weakly; 5 engages the counterargument, updates or holds with reasons.
Verdict guide: average 4 or more and no 1s = would advance; 3 to 4 = borderline; under 3 = not yet.
