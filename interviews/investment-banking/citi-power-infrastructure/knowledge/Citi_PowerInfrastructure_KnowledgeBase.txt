POWER, UTILITIES, INFRASTRUCTURE CONCEPTS (for judging answers)
- Regulated networks: regulator sets an allowed return on the regulated asset base (RAB); value often expressed as a premium or discount to RAB; growth comes from investing in the RAB.
- Revenue types: regulated, contracted (power purchase agreements, contracts for difference, availability payments), and merchant (exposed to power prices).
- Renewables: capacity (MW), capacity factor, generation (MWh), price; cannibalization of prices at times of high solar or wind output.
- Project finance: non-recourse debt sized on cash flow available for debt service (CFADS) and a minimum debt service coverage ratio (DSCR); debt often sculpted to the cash flow profile; high leverage for contracted assets.
- Infrastructure investors: long-term funds accepting lower returns for stable, inflation-linked cash flows; often buy minority stakes in networks or renewables.
- Utilities' financing: credit ratings and net debt / EBITDA or FFO / net debt targets; hybrid bonds receive partial equity credit from rating agencies (often 50 percent); rights issues; asset rotation (sell stakes, recycle capital).
- Valuation: DCF over asset life, sum-of-the-parts for integrated utilities, EV/RAB for networks, EV/EBITDA, dividend yield.

PART D — CORE TECHNICALS WITH ANSWER KEYS (all IB roles)
Ask in increasing difficulty. Assume a 30 percent tax rate unless stated.
======================================================================
T1 Walk me through the three financial statements and how they link.
Answer: income statement (revenue to net income); net income flows to retained earnings in equity on the balance sheet and starts the cash flow statement; cash flow statement adjusts for non-cash items (D&A), working capital, investing (capex), financing (debt, dividends); ending cash goes to the balance sheet.

T2 Depreciation goes up by 10. Walk me through the statements.
Answer: EBIT -10; tax -3; net income -7. Cash flow: net income -7 plus D&A +10 = cash +3. Balance sheet: PP&E -10, cash +3, so assets -7; retained earnings -7. Balances.

T3 Enterprise value vs equity value. Equity value 800, debt 300, cash 100, minority interest 20, pension provisions 50. Enterprise value?
Answer: 800 + 300 - 100 + 20 + 50 = 1,070. Pension provisions are debt-like and matter a lot for German companies.

T4 Which multiples do you use with enterprise value vs equity value, and why?
Answer: EV with metrics before interest (revenue, EBITDA, EBIT), available to all capital providers; equity value with metrics after interest (net income, so P/E). Consistency.

T5 Walk me through a DCF.
Answer: project unlevered free cash flow (EBIT x (1 - tax) + D&A - capex - change in working capital), typically 5 to 10 years; terminal value by perpetuity growth or exit multiple; discount at WACC; sum gives enterprise value; subtract net debt and debt-like items for equity value; divide by diluted shares.

T6 Terminal value. Next year's free cash flow 50, WACC 9 percent, growth 2 percent. Value of that perpetuity today?
Answer: 50 / (0.09 - 0.02) = about 714.
Follow-up: year-5 EBITDA 120 at an 8x exit multiple, discounted five years at 9 percent: 960 / 1.539 = about 624.

T7 WACC. How do you calculate it, and what happens to WACC if a company adds more debt?
Answer: cost of equity (CAPM: risk-free rate + beta x equity risk premium) and after-tax cost of debt, weighted by market values. More debt initially lowers WACC (cheaper, tax-deductible) until rising distress risk raises the costs of debt and equity.

T8 Accretion / dilution. Acquirer net income 100, 100 shares, share price 20 (P/E 20). It buys a target with net income 20 for an equity price of 300. (a) All stock. (b) All debt at 5 percent pre-tax.
Answer: (a) new shares 300 / 20 = 15; EPS 120 / 115 = 1.043, about 4.3 percent accretive (acquirer P/E 20 above price paid P/E 15). (b) after-tax interest 300 x 5 percent x 0.7 = 10.5; EPS (120 - 10.5) / 100 = 1.095, about 9.5 percent accretive. Ignores synergies and fees.

T9 Paper LBO. EBITDA 100, bought at 10x (EV 1,000), 60 percent debt (600), equity 400. EBITDA grows 5 percent a year for five years; exit at 10x; cumulative debt paydown 250. MOIC and IRR?
Answer: exit EBITDA about 127.6; exit EV about 1,276; net debt 350; exit equity about 926; MOIC about 2.3x; IRR about 18 percent. Rules of thumb over five years: 2x is about 15 percent, 2.5x about 20 percent, 3x about 25 percent.

T10 Why might two companies with the same EBITDA trade at different multiples?
Answer: growth, margins and their stability, capex and working-capital needs (cash conversion), risk and cyclicality, market position, quality of management, size and liquidity of the stock.

T11 Comparable companies vs precedent transactions: which gives a higher value and why?
Answer: precedents usually higher because they include a control premium and sometimes synergies; they can also reflect different market conditions.

T12 Locked box vs completion accounts (common in European M&A).
Answer: locked box fixes the price on a historical balance sheet date with no post-closing adjustment, protecting against "leakage" (value going to the seller) after that date; completion accounts adjust the price after closing for actual net debt and working capital. Locked box gives certainty and is seller-friendly.

PART E — POWER AND INFRASTRUCTURE QUESTIONS WITH ANSWER KEYS
======================================================================
S1 Wind farm revenue. 100 MW onshore wind, capacity factor 30 percent, power purchase agreement at 70 euros per MWh. Annual revenue?
Answer: 100 x 8,760 hours x 0.30 = 262,800 MWh; x 70 = about 18.4 million euros.

S2 Debt sizing. Same wind farm: operating costs 5 million a year, so EBITDA about 13.4 million (ignore tax). Lenders require a 1.30x DSCR, 15-year debt at 5 percent with level annual payments. How much debt can it raise? Construction cost 1.5 million euros per MW.
Answer: max annual debt service 13.4 / 1.3 = about 10.3 million. Annuity factor for 15 years at 5 percent is about 10.38. Debt about 107 million. Capex 150 million, so gearing about 71 percent.

S3 A network utility trades at 1.2 times its RAB. What does that mean?
Answer: investors expect returns above the allowed return (outperformance on costs or incentives) and/or value future RAB growth; a premium also reflects low risk and low interest rates.

S4 Contracted vs merchant revenue: how does it change valuation and financing?
Answer: contracted cash flows are more predictable: lower discount rate, higher debt capacity (lower DSCR requirements, longer tenor). Merchant exposure means price risk: higher discount rate and less debt.

S5 Why do utilities issue hybrid bonds?
Answer: rating agencies count part of a hybrid (often 50 percent) as equity, so the company raises money without diluting shareholders and with less rating pressure than senior debt; more expensive than senior debt.

S6 Why do infrastructure funds buy minority stakes in grids?
Answer: long-term, regulated, often inflation-linked returns with low volatility; the utility recycles capital to fund capex while keeping control.

PART F — FINANCING CASE (fictional company)
Introduce the headline in a few sentences. Reveal facts only when asked.
======================================================================
CASE — MAINVOLT (municipal utility funding gap)
Headline: Mainvolt is a German regional utility owned by several municipalities. It runs an electricity grid and owns onshore wind farms. It must invest 3 billion euros in the grid over five years and is worried about its credit rating. The CFO asks us: how big is the funding problem, and what are the options?
Facts on request:
- EBITDA today: grid 400 million, renewables 150 million, total 550 million.
- Net debt today 2.6 billion: about 4.7x net debt / EBITDA.
- Grid capex 3.0 billion over five years. Operating cash flow retained after dividends and tax over the five years: about 1.5 billion.
- Grid EBITDA expected to rise to 520 million in five years as the RAB grows; renewables flat at 150 million.
- To keep its target rating, Mainvolt wants net debt / EBITDA at or below 5.0x (case assumption).
- Options under discussion: sell a 49 percent minority stake in the grid to an infrastructure fund (expected proceeds about 1.5 billion); issue a hybrid bond of 750 million (50 percent equity credit); sell the renewables portfolio (comparable deals around 10x EBITDA); capital increase from the municipalities (their budgets are tight).
- The municipalities want to keep control of the grid.
Answer key:
- Net debt in five years without action: 2.6 + 3.0 - 1.5 = 4.1 billion. EBITDA 520 + 150 = 670 million. Leverage about 6.1x.
- Allowed net debt at 5.0x: 3.35 billion. Gap about 0.75 billion of equity or equity-like funding.
- Grid minority stake: about 1.5 billion fully closes the gap, keeps 51 percent control, but gives away future grid returns and needs governance rights for the fund.
- Hybrid: 750 million counts as about 375 million of equity, so alone it closes only about half the gap; more expensive than senior debt.
- Renewables sale: about 1.5 billion at 10x; closes the gap and removes merchant power-price risk, but gives up the growth story.
- Municipal capital increase: limited by budgets.
- Strong answer: combine options (for example a grid minority stake, or a smaller stake plus a hybrid), weigh control, cost of capital, and strategic story, and mention the process (auction among infrastructure funds, ratings advisory).
