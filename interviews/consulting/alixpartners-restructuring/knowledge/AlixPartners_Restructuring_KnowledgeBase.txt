Interview format commonly reported by candidates (confirm with the recruiter):

- Consultant round with a candidate-led case plus behavioral questions.
- Final round with senior colleagues, two to three cases; some candidates report a psychometric assessment.

What a strong "why restructuring / why AlixPartners" answer references (debrief only):
- Wants real impact under pressure: companies where jobs and survival are at stake.
- Understands the work: cash, operations, stakeholders (banks, owners, employees, customers), often on site with the client.
- Honest about intensity and how they handle it, with evidence.
Weak: "it's countercyclical", "I like finance", anything that would fit any consultancy.

German restructuring background (for context only; not legal advice, and do not expect interns to know it):
- Insolvency grounds: illiquidity (Zahlungsunfähigkeit), over-indebtedness (Überschuldung, with a going-concern forecast), and imminent illiquidity (drohende Zahlungsunfähigkeit) as a voluntary ground.
- Management has short legal deadlines to file once insolvency grounds exist, and personal liability risk.
- Tools: out-of-court restructuring with an independent restructuring opinion (often to IDW S6 standard), the StaRUG restructuring framework (pre-insolvency, can bind dissenting creditors through a plan), self-administration and protective-shield proceedings, and regular insolvency.
- Insolvency money (Insolvenzgeld) from the labor agency covers employees' wages for up to three months before an insolvency opening, which helps liquidity in a formal process.

PART D — RESTRUCTURING FUNDAMENTALS AND QUANT BANK WITH ANSWER KEYS
======================================================================
Q1 Cash vs profit. How can a profitable company go insolvent?
Answer: profit is not cash. Working capital build-up (receivables, inventory), capex, debt repayments, interest, and timing mismatches drain cash; a company becomes insolvent when it can't pay debts as they fall due, not when it makes a loss.

Q2 13-week cash flow. What is it and how do you build it?
Answer: a weekly, direct-method forecast of receipts and disbursements for the next 13 weeks: opening cash, customer receipts, payroll, suppliers, rent, taxes, interest and debt service, capex, closing cash, plus undrawn credit lines, minus a minimum operating cash buffer, giving available liquidity. Built bottom-up with treasury, AR, and AP; variance-tracked every week against actuals.

Q3 Liquidity runway. Cash 12 million, undrawn revolving credit facility 8 million, minimum operating cash 5 million, weekly net burn 1.5 million. How many weeks of runway?
Answer: available liquidity 12 + 8 - 5 = 15 million; 15 / 1.5 = 10 weeks.

Q4 Working capital release. Revenue 365 million (1 million per day), cost of goods sold 292 million (0.8 million per day). DSO 60, DIO 90, DPO 30. Targets: DSO 45, DIO 75, DPO 40. How much cash is released?
Answer: receivables 15 days x 1.0 = 15 million; inventory 15 days x 0.8 = 12 million; payables 10 days x 0.8 = 8 million. Total 35 million. Cash conversion cycle falls from 120 to 80 days. Strong candidates warn that in a crisis suppliers and credit insurers often shorten terms, so the payables lever may run the other way.

Q5 Covenant. Net leverage covenant 3.5x. Net debt 150 million. EBITDA falls from 50 to 35 million. What happens, and how far could EBITDA fall before a breach?
Answer: leverage rises from 3.0x to about 4.3x, a breach. Breach point: 150 / 3.5 = about 42.9 million EBITDA, i.e. a fall of about 14 percent from 50.

Q6 Recovery waterfall. Enterprise value 120 million. Senior secured debt 100 million, junior debt 50 million. What does each class recover?
Answer: senior 100 percent; junior 20 of 50 = 40 percent; equity zero. The junior debt is the "fulcrum" security, the class most likely to convert into equity in a restructuring.

Q7 Restructuring toolbox. What levers exist?
Answer (strong answers group them): liquidity (cash committee, payment prioritization, working capital, asset sales, sale-and-leaseback, factoring, customer support), operational (cost-out, footprint consolidation, procurement, pricing, portfolio), financial (standstill, covenant reset, amend and extend, new money, debt-for-equity swap, sale of the business), formal procedures as a last resort or a tool.

Q8 Cost-out. Overhead is 14 percent of revenue; peers are at 10 percent; revenue 500 million. What's the theoretical savings potential, and why might you not get all of it?
Answer: 4 percent x 500 = 20 million. Not all achievable: structural differences (more countries, more complex products), one-off costs, severance, the need to keep capabilities for the turnaround.

CASE 1 — RHEINGUSS AUTOMOTIVE (liquidity crisis)
Headline: Rheinguss is a German die-casting supplier making engine and transmission housings for car makers. Revenue 480 million euros, EBITDA fell from 40 million to 5 million in a year. The CFO calls on Sunday night: the banks are nervous and he isn't sure the company makes it through the quarter. What do you do in the first week, and will they run out of cash?
Facts on request:
- Cash today: 9 million euros. Revolving credit facility fully drawn.
- Weekly receipts about 9.0 million, weekly disbursements about 9.8 million: net burn 0.8 million per week.
- Quarterly interest payment of 4 million in week 6. Tooling capex payment of 6 million in week 9.
- Minimum operating cash: 4 million.
- Net debt 180 million; covenant test at quarter end.
- 12 million of overdue receivables from one car maker, stuck in a price dispute.
- Inventory at 95 days vs about 60 days for peers. Cost of goods sold about 400 million a year.
- Three car makers are 70 percent of revenue. 60 percent of revenue is combustion-engine parts.
- 2,400 employees at three German plants; plant C runs at 55 percent utilization.
Answer key:
- Cash path: end of week 5: 9 - 4.0 = 5.0 million. Week 6: 9 - 4.8 - 4 = 0.2 million, already below the 4 million minimum. Week 9: 9 - 7.2 - 4 - 6 = -8.2 million. Week 13: 9 - 10.4 - 4 - 6 = -11.4 million. Funding gap including minimum cash: about 15.4 million.
- Week one: set up a 13-week cash flow and a daily cash committee; stop non-essential payments; check insolvency grounds with legal counsel (management has filing duties); talk to banks early (standstill, interest deferral); talk to the car makers.
- Levers within 13 weeks: settle the 12 million dispute (even a partial settlement helps), ask the car maker to pay the tooling (customers often fund tooling), defer interest under a standstill, inventory reduction (95 to 70 days on about 1.1 million COGS per day frees about 27 million over time, perhaps a third within 13 weeks), customer support agreements (price increases, faster payment), factoring, sale-and-leaseback.
- Medium term: consolidate plant C, cost-out, strategy for the shift away from combustion-engine parts, independent restructuring opinion for the banks.
Strong candidates identify the week-6 crunch, quantify the gap, and put stakeholder management (banks, car makers) alongside internal cash measures.

CASE 2 — MODEHAUS FELDMANN (retail profitability turnaround)
Headline: Feldmann is a German fashion retailer with 120 stores. Revenue 300 million euros, falling 8 percent a year; EBITDA minus 6 million. The owners ask for a plan to get back to positive EBITDA within 18 months.
Facts on request:
- Store profitability (four-wall EBITDA): 40 stores clearly profitable, 50 around break-even, 30 loss-making at about minus 0.4 million each (minus 12 million in total).
- The 30 loss-making stores generate 45 million of revenue. About 20 percent of their sales would transfer to nearby stores or online if closed.
- Variable contribution on transferred sales: about 30 percent.
- Closing a store costs about 0.5 million one-off (lease exit, severance).
- Rent is 18 percent of sales. Online is 10 percent of sales.
- Head-office overhead 30 million (10 percent of sales); peers about 7 to 8 percent.
- Markdowns 18 percent of sales vs about 12 percent at peers.
- Cash: 10 million, no undrawn lines.
Answer key:
- Close 30 stores: removes minus 12 million four-wall loss, plus transferred sales 9 million x 30 percent = 2.7 million: EBITDA from minus 6 to about plus 8.7 million.
- Head office at 8 percent of remaining revenue (300 - 45 + 9 = 264 million): about 21.1 million vs 30, saving about 8.9 million. Total about plus 17.6 million before markdown improvements.
- Markdowns: better buying and less inventory can save several points of margin over time.
- The catch: closures cost 30 x 0.5 = 15 million cash, more than the 10 million they have. Options: phase closures, negotiate with landlords, owner or lender bridge funding, or a formal process (which can make lease exits cheaper but risks supplier credit insurance and reputation).
Strong candidates segment stores, quantify, and spot the cash funding problem.

CASE 3 — NORDBAU SYSTEMS (financial restructuring)
Headline: Nordbau is a building-materials company owned by a private-equity fund. EBITDA fell from 90 to 60 million euros. Net debt is 420 million, the term loan matures in 14 months, and the fund asks: what are our options?
Facts on request:
- Debt: senior term loan 300 million, second-lien loan 120 million.
- Leverage: 420 / 60 = 7.0x.
- Comparable companies trade around 6.5x EBITDA, so enterprise value is about 390 million.
- The fund is willing to put in 30 million of new equity, but only if lenders also contribute.
- Business plan: EBITDA back to 75 million in two years if construction volumes recover.
Answer key:
- Waterfall at 390 million: senior 300 fully covered; second lien recovers 90 of 120 = 75 percent; equity is out of the money. The second lien is the fulcrum.
- Options: amend and extend with the senior lenders (needs a credible plan); new money from the fund; debt-for-equity swap in which the second-lien lenders take a majority of the equity; sale of the business; a StaRUG plan if lenders don't agree.
- A likely solution: senior extended, second lien converts largely into equity, fund injects new money for a minority stake, leverage falls to about 300 / 60 = 5.0x.
Strong candidates identify who holds the value (the fulcrum) and frame options from each stakeholder's view.
