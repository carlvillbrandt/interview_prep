2025 financial year: about 606 million euros revenue (+6 percent), about 2,200 employees, 45 offices, people in 32 countries, clients in more than 50 markets. 28 new partners from January 1, 2026, for 227 partners in total.
Practices: pricing and monetization, commercial strategy, growth / sales and marketing, revenue management, go-to-market execution, digitalization, commercial due diligence and value creation for investors.
Known ideas associated with co-founder Hermann Simon: "Hidden Champions" (little-known world-market leaders, often German Mittelstand) and the book "Confessions of the Pricing Man".

Interview format commonly reported by candidates (offices vary — the candidate should confirm with the recruiter):
- CV and motivation screen; in some offices an online numerical/logical test.
- First round: pricing case plus behavioral questions.
- Later round: senior interviewers, deeper pricing and monetization cases.
- Cases are candidate-led but stay on pricing. Generic profitability trees on a pricing problem are reported to land badly.

What a strong "why Simon-Kucher" answer references (debrief only):
- Specialization: price is usually the strongest profit lever, and a pricing specialist sees many industries through that one lens.
- Concrete interest: a pricing model the candidate finds clever or broken, and why.
- Link to their own background (sales, startups, B2B industry, software, economics).
Weak: "you're the market leader", "I like numbers", anything that would fit any consultancy.

PART B — HOW TO PROBE A CANDIDATE'S BACKGROUND
======================================================================
Pick the one or two CV items most relevant to this role. Never recite the CV. For each item choose two or three probes.

ANY ROLE
- What did you personally own or decide, as opposed to the team?
- What was the result, in a number?
- What would you do differently?
- How does this make you better at pricing and commercial strategy?

CONSULTING, ADVISORY, STRATEGY
- Which project taught you the most about how a business makes money?
- How did you structure the problem on day one? What did the first hypothesis look like, and was it right?
- What did the client actually change because of your work?

SALES, PRICING, NEGOTIATION, BUSINESS DEVELOPMENT
- Walk me through one negotiation. What was your walk-away point, and how did you know?
- How was the product priced, and what would you have changed?

BANKING, PE, VC, TRANSACTION WORK
- Walk me through one deal or diligence you worked on. What was the key commercial question?
- Where did your analysis change someone's mind?

STARTUP OR OPERATOR EXPERIENCE (employee or founder)
- What was the company's biggest problem, and what did you do about it?
- Founders: who paid, how much, and why did they choose you over alternatives?

STUDENT CLUBS, CONSULTING CLUBS, COMMUNITY BUILDING
- What did you build or change, not just join?
- Tell me about a time the team disagreed. What did you do?

RESEARCH, TECHNICAL, OR ENGINEERING BACKGROUND
- Explain your work to a client CEO in 30 seconds.
- How would you apply that analytical approach to a business problem?

NO DIRECTLY RELEVANT EXPERIENCE OR CAREER SWITCHER
- What made you decide on this path, and what have you done to test that decision?
- What do you bring that a typical candidate doesn't?

PART C — MOTIVATION AND FIT QUESTION BANK
======================================================================
1. Walk me through your background in two minutes.
   Strong: crisp, chronological, each step explains the next, ends with why consulting and pricing now.
2. Why pricing, and why not a generalist strategy consultancy?
   Strong: understands what pricing work is (value analysis, willingness-to-pay research, price models, sales enablement, implementation); a concrete example that sparked interest.
3. Tell me about a product whose pricing you find clever — and one you'd fix.
   Strong: names the pricing metric, segments, tiers, psychology; explains who captures value and why.
4. Tell me about a time you persuaded someone who disagreed with you.
   Strong: situation, their objection, what evidence or framing changed it, result.
5. Tell me about a failure and what you changed afterward.
6. How do you handle a client who says "we can't raise prices, customers will leave"?
   Strong: empathize, then evidence: segments with different price sensitivity, value delivered, test-and-learn, competitor moves, sales enablement.

======================================================================
PART D — PRICING CONCEPTS AND QUANT BANK WITH ANSWER KEYS
======================================================================
Concepts the candidate may use (judge correct use, not jargon):
- Value-based pricing vs cost-plus vs competition-based.
- Willingness to pay (WTP); research methods: Van Westendorp price sensitivity meter (four questions: too cheap, cheap/bargain, expensive, too expensive; acceptable range between curve intersections), conjoint analysis (trade-offs between features and price), Gabor-Granger (purchase likelihood at price points), A/B price tests.
- Price metric (what the price is attached to: per user, per order, per machine hour, per outcome).
- Good-better-best tiers, bundling, decoy effect, anchoring, freemium, usage-based and subscription models.
- Price waterfall: list price minus discounts and rebates down to the pocket price actually realized.
- Price elasticity: percentage change in volume divided by percentage change in price.

Q1 Price as profit lever. Revenue 100 million, variable costs 60 million, fixed costs 30 million, profit 10 million. What is the profit impact of a 1 percent improvement in price, volume, variable cost, and fixed cost (each alone)?
Answer: price +1 percent: +1.0 million = +10 percent profit. Volume +1 percent: contribution +0.4 million = +4 percent. Variable cost -1 percent: +0.6 million = +6 percent. Fixed cost -1 percent: +0.3 million = +3 percent. Point: price is the strongest lever.

Q2 Break-even volume for a price cut. Price 100 euros, variable cost 60 euros, contribution margin 40 percent. The sales team wants a 10 percent price cut. How much must volume rise to keep total contribution flat?
Answer: new unit contribution 30 vs 40, so volume must rise by 40/30 - 1 = 33.3 percent. Formula: required volume change = price cut / (margin - price cut) = 10 / (40 - 10).
Follow-up: a 10 percent price increase lets volume fall by 1 - 40/50 = 20 percent before contribution falls.

Q3 Elasticity. Price elasticity is -1.5. Price goes up 10 percent; price 100, variable cost 60. What happens to volume, revenue, and contribution?
Answer: volume about -15 percent; revenue 1.10 x 0.85 = 0.935, so about -6.5 percent; unit contribution rises from 40 to 50, so total contribution 50 x 0.85 = 42.5 vs 40, about +6.25 percent. Point: revenue falls but profit rises.

Q4 Price waterfall. List price 100. Invoice discount 15, off-invoice rebate 5, early-payment discount 2, free freight worth 3 (all per unit, in euros). What is the pocket price and the leakage?
Answer: pocket price 75; leakage 25 percent of list. Strong candidates ask which customers get which discounts and whether discounts correlate with volume (often they don't).

Q5 Subscription. Monthly plan 20 euros. Annual plan 192 euros. What discount does the annual plan give, and why would a company offer it?
Answer: 240 vs 192 = 20 percent discount. Reasons: lower churn, cash upfront, lock-in; cost: lower revenue per committed user.

Q6 Market sizing (approach only, no single right number): How many e-bikes are sold in Germany per year?
Strong approach: population or households, share of cyclists, share buying a new bike per year, e-bike share of new bikes; or replacement cycle of the e-bike stock; sanity check. Industry figures in recent years have been roughly two million a year; reward the logic, not the exact number.

CASE 1 — BRENNTAL DRUCKLUFT (industrial, pay-per-use)
Headline: Brenntal is a German Mittelstand manufacturer of industrial air compressors. Margins are under pressure from Asian competitors. The CEO asks: should we stop selling compressors and instead sell "compressed air as a service," charging per cubic meter? And if so, at what price?
Facts on request:
- Today: compressor list price 40,000 euros; Brenntal's manufacturing cost 28,000 euros.
- Optional service contract: 3,000 euros per year to the customer; Brenntal's cost to deliver 2,000 euros per year.
- Compressor lifetime: 10 years.
- Customer's electricity cost to run one compressor: 25,000 euros per year (energy is by far the largest lifetime cost).
- One compressor produces about 1.6 million cubic meters of compressed air per year at a typical customer.
- Brenntal's new sensors and software cut energy use by 15 percent if Brenntal operates the machine.
- In the as-a-service model Brenntal owns, services, and pays the electricity for the compressor, and charges per cubic meter.
- Customers: mid-sized manufacturers; CFOs like predictable costs and avoiding capex.
Answer key:
- Customer's current cost over 10 years: 40,000 + 30,000 service + 250,000 energy = 320,000, i.e. 32,000 per year, or 2.0 cents per cubic meter (32,000 / 1.6 million). This is the price ceiling (customer indifference).
- Brenntal's annual cost in the service model: 2,800 depreciation (28,000 / 10) + 2,000 service + 21,250 energy (25,000 x 0.85) = 26,050 per year, about 1.63 cents per cubic meter. This is the price floor.
- A price around 1.9 cents per cubic meter: customer pays 30,400 per year and saves 5 percent; Brenntal earns 4,350 per year, 43,500 over 10 years, vs 22,000 today (12,000 hardware margin + 10,000 service margin). Roughly double the lifetime profit per machine.
- Risks: electricity price (fix with an energy index clause), customer volume falling (minimum volume or base fee plus usage), Brenntal's balance sheet must finance 28,000 per machine upfront (consider a leasing partner), sales team incentives change from one-off to recurring.
Strong recommendation: yes for the segment that values predictable opex, priced between floor and ceiling with a base fee plus per-cubic-meter rate and an energy index clause; keep outright purchase as an option.

CASE 2 — LAGERLICHT (B2B software, pricing metric)
Headline: Lagerlicht sells warehouse-management software to mid-sized logistics companies at 50 euros per user per month. As warehouses automate, customers need fewer users and revenue is shrinking. Lagerlicht asks how to redesign its price model.
Facts on request:
- 400 customers, on average 40 users each. Annual recurring revenue: 400 x 40 x 50 x 12 = 9.6 million euros.
- Users per customer are falling about 8 percent a year.
- The value customers get scales with orders processed. The average customer processes 500,000 orders per year.
- Annual customer churn: 6 percent.
- Survey: 35 percent of customers would pay 20 percent more for an integration module that connects warehouse robots.
- Customers dislike unpredictable bills; their CFOs want budgets fixed a year ahead.
- Competitors mostly still price per user.
Answer key:
- Revenue-neutral per-order price: 9.6 million / (400 x 500,000 = 200 million orders) = 4.8 cents per order.
- Better structure: platform fee plus per-order usage in volume bands, with annual commitments to keep bills predictable; good-better-best tiers (for example Essentials, Professional, Enterprise with robot integration).
- Robot module: if 35 percent of customers pay 20 percent more, uplift is about 0.35 x 0.20 = 7 percent of revenue, about 0.67 million a year.
- Migration: grandfather existing contracts for a period, move at renewal, guarantee "no customer pays more than X percent more in year one", train sales.
- Risks: customer volumes fall in a recession (usage pricing makes revenue cyclical), sales complexity, competitor undercutting.
Strong recommendation: move from per-user to a value metric (orders) with a platform fee and annual commitments, launch tiers with robot integration in the top tier, migrate at renewal.

CASE 3 — ALPENBAD (consumer, dynamic pricing)
Headline: Alpenbad is a large thermal spa and water park in Bavaria. It charges a flat 30 euros per day ticket. Weekends are overcrowded and people are turned away; weekdays are half empty. Management asks whether to introduce differentiated pricing and what it is worth.
Facts on request:
- Capacity: 3,000 visitors per day.
- Weekend demand at 30 euros: 3,800 visitors per day (800 turned away). 104 weekend days a year.
- Weekday demand at 30 euros: 1,200 visitors per day. 261 weekdays a year.
- Estimated price elasticity: weekend -0.6, weekday -1.8 (use as linear approximations).
- Food and drink: 12 euros spend per visitor at a 60 percent margin.
- Many visitors are locals with season passes (not in scope of this question).
Answer key:
- Weekend at 36 euros (+20 percent): demand 3,800 x (1 - 0.6 x 0.20) = 3,344, still above capacity, so 3,000 visitors. Revenue 108,000 vs 90,000 per day, +18,000 per day x 104 = about 1.87 million euros a year.
- Weekend price that just fills capacity: 3,800 x (1 - 0.6x) = 3,000 gives x = 35 percent, about 40.50 euros; revenue 121,500 per day. Strong candidates note fairness and brand risk at the top end.
- Weekday at 25.50 euros (-15 percent): visitors 1,200 x (1 + 1.8 x 0.15) = 1,524. Ticket revenue 38,862 vs 36,000, +2,862 per day x 261 = about 0.75 million. Extra food and drink: 324 visitors x 12 x 0.6 = about 2,333 per day x 261 = about 0.61 million.
- Total uplift in the 20-percent weekend / 15-percent weekday scenario: about 3.2 million euros a year.
- Risks: perceived unfairness, local backlash, complexity; mitigate with simple two-tier pricing (weekday / weekend), online booking, and communicating "cheaper on weekdays" rather than "surge pricing".

======================================================================
Motivation and fit: 1 generic ("consulting is varied"); 3 reasonable but not pricing-specific; 5 specific, credible interest in pricing tied to their own experience.
Case structure: 1 none or a generic profit tree; 3 sensible but generic; 5 pricing-specific, hypothesis-driven, structure stated upfront.

Quantitative accuracy: 1 wrong and lost; 3 right with help or small errors; 5 fast, correct, explains the implication.
Business judgment: 1 no recommendation; 3 recommendation with some risks; 5 clear recommendation, key risk, implementation step.
Communication: 1 long, rambling; 3 clear but long; 5 answer first, crisp, client-ready.
Verdict guide: average 4 or more and no 1s = would advance; 3 to 4 = borderline; under 3 = not yet.
